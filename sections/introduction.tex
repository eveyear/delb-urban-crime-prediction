\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{The Data-Value Problem in Urban Crime Count Forecasting}
\label{subsec:intro_data_value}

Urban crime forecasting increasingly combines historical incidents with
mobility traces, calendar variables, environmental measurements, and other
dynamic urban data. These sources can provide richer representations of activity
around a location, but they also require acquisition, cleaning, spatial
alignment, maintenance, and governance. For a city agency or an analyst, the
relevant question is not simply whether a fitted model achieves a
lower test error after a new source is added, but whether that source
reduces uncertainty about future crime counts beyond information already
available from location, crime history, and the calendar.

These two questions are not equivalent. A change in held-out error depends on
the fitted model, hyperparameter search, training sample, target definition,
and validation period. It may reflect useful new information, a better match
between the added variables and one model class, or sampling variation. In
contrast, a model-independent prediction benchmark is determined by the joint
distribution of the target and the information available when a forecast is
issued. Comparing achieved error with such a benchmark can help distinguish
information-constrained uncertainty under the specified target and
information set from error that may remain reducible through improved
modeling.

Crime count forecasting makes this issue practically important.
Incident counts are nonnegative integers, are often zero at fine spatial and
temporal resolutions, and are evaluated under losses such as mean squared
error (MSE). \rev{The forecasting literature has progressed from models using
urban metrics \citep{alves2018crime} to representations of temporal
dependence \citep{huang2018deepcrime} and multiscale spatial structure
\citep{jing2024multiscale}. Reviews of spatial forecasting
\citep{kounadi2020review}, multiscale designs \citep{du2023review}, and
machine- and deep-learning methods \citep{mandalapu2023review} show how
widely targets and evaluation settings vary.} Their comparative results are essential for model selection,
but they do not identify how much error is unavoidable under a specified
information set. This limitation becomes more consequential when an
operationally demanding new urban data source appears to improve one or more
fitted models.

\rev{We study this problem using shared-bicycle activity as a candidate source
of additional information. The motivation is observational rather than
mechanistic: station departures and arrivals provide high-frequency,
spatially resolved traces of one form of local street activity and exposure.
They may therefore help describe when and where potential targets, guardians,
or other users of public space are present beyond what recent crime and the
calendar already reveal. We do not assume that bicycle users cause crime, that
offenders travel by bicycle, or that bicycle flow measures the whole ambient
population. The empirical question is whether lagged flow contains incremental
forecast-time information about subsequent property-crime counts and whether
an estimated gain can be distinguished from finite-sample artifacts.}

\subsection{From Integer-Valued Uncertainty to Calibrated Evidence}
\label{subsec:intro_calibrated_evidence}

Conditional entropy provides a natural measure of the uncertainty remaining
after the forecast-time information set has been observed
\citep{shannon1948,cover_thomas}. Existing entropy-based prediction bounds,
however, are mainly formulated for continuous outcomes or classification
losses. A continuous Gaussian entropy--variance argument cannot be transferred
to integer-valued crime counts by simply replacing differential entropy with
Shannon entropy. The prediction error lies on an integer lattice, and the
relevant maximum-entropy envelope under a second-moment constraint is
discrete.

To address this support mismatch, we define the Discrete Entropy Lower
Bound (DELB). DELB maps conditional Shannon entropy through the inverse
integer-lattice maximum-entropy envelope to a model-independent lower bound on
\rev{the expected squared one-step forecast error,
\(\mathbb E[(X_t-\widehat X_t)^2]\),} for forecast-time causal, or
non-anticipating, integer-valued predictors. Here, ``causal'' refers only to the temporal
admissibility of information available when a forecast is issued; it does not
denote identification of a causal effect. The envelope inversion is exact for
the stated integer-lattice maximum-entropy problem, although equality with
the optimal integer-predictor risk requires the stated attainability
conditions. The framework also includes a predictor-dependent refinement and
separates two sources of nonattainment: residual dependence of the prediction
error on the available history and mismatch between the error distribution
and the moment-matched discrete Gaussian.

The discrete-Gaussian maximum-entropy law and lattice entropy corrections are
established results. Here, they are adapted to non-anticipating integer
prediction and linked to predictor-specific attainability conditions,
incremental information analysis, and finite-sample calibration.

A population ordering addresses only the first part of the applied problem. Adding
a discretized mobility variable refines the baseline state space, often
creating sparse or singleton cells. Conditional-entropy and conditional
mutual information (CMI) estimators can then have different finite-sample
errors in the baseline and augmented state tables
\citep{paninski2003,calcagnile2026benchmark}. A positive estimated CMI, or a
lower estimated DELB, may consequently arise even when the added variable has
no population information value. A randomization reference is also
conditioned on its transformation design and need not be centered at zero
when that design changes support occupancy. Permutation inference requires an
explicit transformation design \citep{ernst2004permutation}. CMI-based
conditional-independence methods use, respectively, nearest-neighbor
estimation \citep{runge2018cmi}, mixed-data estimation \citep{zan2022cmi},
and nearest-neighbor sampling \citep{li2023nncit}.

The evaluation must therefore distinguish four objects: the population
DELB and CMI estimands, their finite-sample estimates, evidence relative to a
prespecified randomization reference, and realized out-of-sample prediction
error. We connect these layers using known-truth simulations, support
diagnostics, block resampling, temporal and spatial randomization,
circular-shift references, and matched prediction models. This design does
not turn a transformation-specific diagnostic into a causal or exact
conditional-independence test. Its purpose is to determine whether the
observed statistic is distinguishable from finite-sample behavior under each
explicit reference design.

\subsection{Research Questions and Contributions}
\label{subsec:intro_contributions}

The study addresses three linked research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{How can conditional uncertainty be translated into a
    discrete-form, model-independent MSE lower bound for non-anticipating
    integer-valued prediction?}
    \item \textit{How can a positive CMI estimate be separated from
    sparse-state estimation bias and a transformation-specific randomization
    reference?}
    \item \textit{Under this calibrated framework, what evidence does lagged
    bicycle mobility provide about future urban crime counts, and when is
    that information realized by fitted prediction models?}
\end{enumerate}

\rev{The study makes three contributions. First, it organizes established
integer-lattice maximum-entropy results into an explicit DELB framework for
non-anticipating integer prediction, together with attainability and
nonattainment diagnostics.} Second, it develops a finite-sample
procedure for assessing whether an apparent reduction in the bound can be
distinguished from sparse-state estimation effects. Third, it applies the
procedure to harmonized crime and shared-bicycle data from three cities and
compares the estimated bounds with matched held-out prediction errors.

\rev{Adding lagged bicycle flow lowers the estimated DELB in both city-level
analysis populations. In the primary bicycle-covered, grid-identity
specification, however, the observed CMI is not distinguishable from the
prespecified temporal, spatial,
circular-shift, or baseline-state-matched references after calibration.
In a lower-cardinality sensitivity, only New York City shows a small
matched-reference difference after multiplicity adjustment; this finding
qualifies the primary non-rejection and demonstrates the dependence of
finite-sample resolution on state design.} Realized prediction changes are model- and city-dependent. The empirical
application therefore serves as
a calibration case study in which the estimated DELB reduction,
randomization-based evidence, and predictive performance are evaluated
separately.
