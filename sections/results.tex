\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first report the city-level DELB estimates and then examine finite-sample
bias, randomization calibration, and held-out prediction. Multiscale,
detectability, Fano, heterogeneity, and rolling analyses are presented as
supporting checks.

\subsection{Multicity Application Results at a Glance}
\label{subsec:application_results_overview}

\input{sections/table_application_results_overview}

Adding lagged bicycle flow lowered the DELB point estimate in all three
cities, but neither the randomization analyses nor the prediction models
showed a uniform gain (Table~\ref{tab:application_results_overview}). In the complete
crime-supported deployment populations, adding lagged bicycle flow yielded
positive but small estimated DELB reductions. In the bicycle-covered
populations, the corresponding relative reductions were 7.9\% in Washington,
DC, 17.3\% in New York City, and 18.0\% in Vancouver. The contrast was largest
in New York City and Vancouver.

\rev{Neither the three transformation references nor the
baseline-state-matched reference separated the observed CMI in any city under
the primary grid-identity specification. A lower-cardinality sensitivity
detected only a small New York difference after multiplicity adjustment.}
Held-out prediction was also selective: multiplicity-adjusted improvement
tests were positive for two of four New York City models and one Vancouver
model, whereas only the two New York City models narrowed the estimated
Predictability Gap. Thus, lower DELB point estimates coexisted with
non-rejection in the randomization analyses and heterogeneous held-out
performance.

\subsection{Estimated DELB Changes}
\label{subsec:results_city_information}

For the bicycle-covered population used by the detailed calibration
workflow, adding the one-day-lagged bicycle state reduced estimated
conditional entropy in all three cities. The Miller--Madow CMI
estimates were \DCCMI{} bits in Washington, DC, \NYCMI{} bits in New York City,
and \VANCMI{} bits in Vancouver.
Mapping the baseline and bicycle-augmented conditional entropies through the
same inverse integer-lattice envelope produced estimated DELB reductions of
\DCDeltaL{} MSE units in Washington, DC, \NYDeltaL{} in New York City, and
\VANDeltaL{} in Vancouver. These changes represented relative reductions of
\DCRelativeL, \NYRelativeL, and \VANRelativeL, respectively
(Figure~\ref{fig:city_information_bounds}).
The centered seven-day block-bootstrap intervals for the CMI estimates
excluded zero. These intervals quantify sampling variation under the
observed state partition and dependence structure; randomization-based
attribution is examined separately in Section~\ref{subsec:results_nulls}.

The two training-period-frozen analysis populations retained
positive city-level point estimates.
For the deployment population, CMI was 0.0420, 0.0348, and 0.0377 bits in
Washington, DC, New York City, and Vancouver, respectively, compared with
0.0432, 0.1314, and 0.1423 bits for the bicycle-covered population. The
corresponding deployment-population DELB reductions were 0.0119, 0.0094, and
0.0115 MSE units, versus 0.0123, 0.0604, and 0.0919 in the bicycle-covered
populations. \rev{The covered shares were 0.973, 0.246, and 0.241. Multiplying
covered-domain CMI by these shares reproduced the Washington deployment
estimate and yielded 0.0323 and 0.0343 bits for New York and Vancouver,
leaving residuals of 0.0025 and 0.0034 bits. Because grid identity is in the
conditioning state, the complete-domain CMI decomposes exactly into the
observation-share-weighted CMIs inside and outside the frozen footprint.
Direct estimation gives outside-footprint CMI of 0.00000, 0.00333, and
0.00445 bits in Washington, New York, and Vancouver; multiplying by the
outside shares yields residual contributions of 0.00000, 0.00251, and
0.00338 bits. These values account for the 8--10\% arithmetic difference
in the two low-coverage cities. Separately, 2022 bicycle activity occurred
outside the frozen training footprint (212 New York grids and 11 Vancouver
grids), consistent with nondegenerate outside-footprint states. This
decomposition identifies the population contributing the residual, not a
causal mechanism or a share attributable specifically to 2022 activity.}
Thus, the sign was stable, while
the magnitude difference in New York City and Vancouver was primarily a
coverage-weighting effect. This paired comparison concerns city-level point
estimates only; the downstream
inferential experiments retain their prespecified bicycle-covered
populations.
\rev{A third set of DELB differences, 0.01511, 0.03363, and 0.05095 MSE
units, was re-estimated on the covered July--December 2022 test window only
to pair the bound with held-out model error. It is not a third estimate for
the full 2020--2022 population. Supplementary Table S11 places these three
populations, periods, and uses side by side.}

Local estimates were positive across most eligible cells, with detectable
spatial clustering in Washington, DC and Vancouver but not New York City.
Map colors represent point-estimate magnitude rather than calibrated local
evidence.

\subsection{Finite-Sample Behavior of CMI Estimation}
\label{subsec:results_e12_simulation}

Known-truth simulations were used to determine how sparse nested states
affect CMI estimation and
transformation-based calibration. Population CMI was obtained
by exact enumeration, so estimator bias could be separated from the
center of the corresponding randomization distribution.

Under the 72 population-null design cells, both estimators produced
positive CMI estimates despite the true CMI being zero. Mean null bias
was \SimPluginNullBias{} bits for the plug-in estimator and \SimMMNullBias{} bits
for the Miller--Madow estimator. Increasing the sample size from 365 to 1095
observations reduced average bias, whereas larger joint state spaces,
greater singleton occupancy, and larger effective conditional degrees
of freedom increased it. The Miller--Madow correction reduced average
bias but did not uniformly outperform the plug-in estimator in the
sparsest designs.

The transformation-specific reference distributions were also positively centered.
Their mean centers were \SimPluginNullCenter{} bits for the plug-in estimator
and \SimMMNullCenter{} bits for the Miller--Madow estimator. Despite these nonzero centers,
the mean Type I rejection rates were \SimPluginTypeI{} and \SimMMTypeI{},
respectively, because the observed statistics were compared with their
complete transformation-specific reference distributions rather than with
zero. Mean power across the positive-CMI designs was \SimPluginPower{} for
the plug-in estimator and \SimMMPower{} for the Miller--Madow estimator (Table~\ref{tab:e12_simulation_summary}
and Figure~\ref{fig:e12_sparse_simulation}).

Support diagnostics showed consistent associations with finite-sample
behavior. In the known-null simulations, observed-atom density,
singleton-observation share, and normalized effective degrees of freedom
were associated with the bias of both estimators. Across the 1017 empirical
city--grid--reference summaries, their Spearman correlations with the
randomization-reference mean were 0.835, 0.866, and 0.988, respectively.
In a fixed-effects model with city-grid-clustered standard errors,
surrogate normalized effective degrees of freedom had a coefficient of
\SupportCombinedNuCoefficient{} (SE \SupportCombinedNuSE{};
\(R^2=\SupportCombinedNullRSquared{}\)).

The transformations also changed the CMI support.
Changes in support complexity between each surrogate and the observed
table were negatively associated with observed-minus-reference CMI, with correlations ranging
from \(-0.653\) to \(-0.791\). These relations are diagnostic rather than
causal and do not define a universal bias correction. They show that the
center of a finite-sample randomization distribution depends jointly on
the estimator, observed support, and transformation design. Accordingly,
the calibrated analyses below compare observed CMI with complete
design-matched reference distributions rather than using zero as an
automatic benchmark.



\subsection{Randomization Calibration and Lag Specificity}
\label{subsec:results_nulls}

The randomization analyses evaluated whether the observed CMI was unusually
large relative to three prespecified transformation-based reference
distributions. Evidence under a given design required the observed
statistic to fall in the upper tail of that reference distribution;
the reference center was not constrained to zero.

None of the nine city-by-reference comparisons rejected its corresponding
upper-tail null. Unadjusted \(p\)-values ranged from 0.903 to 1.000, and all
multiplicity-adjusted \(q\)-values were 1.000. In most comparisons, the observed CMI was below
the corresponding reference mean. At the local level, none of the 339
eligible cells passed all three randomization designs, including cells
that had passed the block-bootstrap sampling-uncertainty screen.

The estimator-sensitivity analysis produced the same city-level conclusion
for all three entropy estimators. Across the nine comparisons within each estimator, raw
\(p\)-values ranged from 0.980 to 1.000 for plug-in, 0.903 to 1.000 for
Miller--Madow, and 0.984 to 1.000 for Jeffreys--Dirichlet. No comparison
passed either the estimator-specific nine-test correction
(\rev{\ESeventeenWithinRejects{}/9}) or the global 27-test correction
(\ESeventeenGlobalRejects{}/27), consistently across estimator-specific null
summaries.

The distant-lag analysis produced similar CMI estimates. Ratios of
14-day- or 30-day-lag CMI to the matched one-day-lag CMI ranged from
\DistantRatioMin{} to \DistantRatioMax{}. Both distant-lag estimates
exceeded the one-day estimate in New York City, whereas differences
in Washington, DC and Vancouver were small and inconsistent
(Table~\ref{tab:null_calibration}).

\rev{The similarity of one-, 14-, and 30-day estimates is more consistent
with slow-moving exposure, seasonality, or another persistent common driver
than with information specific to bicycle activity on the preceding day.}
\rev{The complete local eligibility statistics, maps, and detailed lag figure
are retained as secondary diagnostics.}

The randomization and distant-lag comparisons did not isolate an information
signal specific to recent, geographically matched bicycle activity, although
this conclusion remains conditional on the transformations examined.

\input{sections/table_e18_restricted_calibration}

The baseline-state-matched restricted randomization reached a movable-block
share of 1.00 in every city and preserved the bicycle-state marginal
exactly. Its null means were again slightly above the observed CMI
(Table~\ref{tab:e18_restricted_calibration}). The upper-tail \(p\)-values
were 0.599, 0.999, and 0.994 in Washington, DC, New York City, and
Vancouver; all three BH-adjusted \(q\)-values were 0.999. Thus, bringing
the reference closer to the baseline state did not reverse the
non-rejection under the broader transformation designs.

{\color{red}\sloppy Table~\ref{tab:revision_calibration} places this result beside observed
support diagnostics and the lower-cardinality sensitivity. Replacing grid
identity with training-only crime-intensity quintiles reduced
\(K_{obs}/N\) from 0.236--0.412 to 0.032--0.180. Washington and Vancouver
remained near their matched-reference centers. New York CMI was 0.03625 bits,
0.00105 bits above the 999-surrogate mean (plus-one \(p=0.001\),
three-city BH \(q=0.003\)). The magnitude is small and does not validate the
larger primary point estimate; it shows that state complexity controls
finite-sample resolution.\par}

\rev{Two higher-complexity sensitivities provided no calibrated separation.
Replacing season with calendar month produced observed-minus-null differences
of -0.00287, -0.01623, and -0.02482 bits; using zero plus positive-flow
quintiles produced -0.00211, -0.00272, and -0.00864 bits in Washington, New
York, and Vancouver, respectively.}

The semi-synthetic results qualified that finding. Under zero population
CMI, restricted-design rejection rates ranged from 0.00 to 0.08 across
cities and dispersion settings, and every exact 95\% binomial interval
covered 0.05. However, power at 0.02 and 0.05 bit was only 0.00--0.10.
Even under the fitted-dispersion scenario, power at the highest evaluated
attainable CMI was 0.48 in Washington, DC, 0.46 in New York City, and
0.30 in Vancouver. Some requested high-CMI settings were unattainable on
the fixed empirical support and were reported at their actual maxima.
The semi-synthetic analysis therefore supports calibration of the restricted
reference but also shows limited detection power; non-rejection cannot establish
conditional irrelevance.

\subsubsection{Spatiotemporal Scale and Sample-Size Calibration}
\label{subsec:e16_scale_results}

The multiscale analysis separated target changes caused by aggregation from
within-design stability and randomization calibration. All 18 full-sample
city--space--time combinations yielded positive estimated DELB reductions,
and estimates became more stable as the retained time-block fraction
increased. Nevertheless, none of the 270
city--space--time--sample--reference tests survived global or within-family
FDR correction (minimum raw \(p=\ScaleMinimumRawP{}\); minimum within-family
\(q=\ScaleMinimumWithinQ{}\)). Because aggregation changes the outcome and
MSE unit, these results do not imply a scale-invariant effect.

\rev{The smallest isolated value was \(p=0.006\) for the New York City
500~m--day circular-shift comparison, but it did not survive the prespecified
FDR correction. Across the 90 full-sample design cells, 21 circular-shift
statistics exceeded their reference means compared with none under the
temporal-block design. This asymmetry shows that the reference mechanisms
remove different portions of temporal and spatial structure and should not be
treated as interchangeable tests of one universal null.}


\subsubsection{Truth-Referenced Convergence and Detectability}
\label{subsec:e19_identifiability_results}

The known-truth experiment showed that increasing the generated record
length generally reduced CMI estimation error, but it did not produce a
single stability threshold shared by all scales and estimators
(Figure~\ref{fig:e19_truth_convergence}). Across positive-CMI cells and the
three estimators, median CMI RMSE was 0.159 bits at \(N=90\), 0.167 at
\(N=150\), 0.135 at \(N=365\), and 0.119 at \(N=730\). At the 2~km--week
scale, the corresponding scale-specific median declined from 0.266 to
0.152 bits. DELB relative RMSE was less monotone: its overall median was
0.734, 0.666, 0.432, and 0.521 over the same four sample lengths. Thus,
more generated periods improved CMI recovery overall, whereas nonlinear
entropy-to-DELB inversion and sparse support prevented a universal
convergence rule.

\rev{The two power studies answer different questions. The earlier
empirical-support experiment uses six selected grids per city and the fitted
city supports to qualify the primary city non-rejections; it is therefore the
direct governing power analysis for those results. The scale--sample
experiment varies record length and aggregation under known injected CMI to
study design resolution. In the broader sparse-state simulation, Miller--Madow
Type~I rejection averaged 0.011 and plug-in rejection averaged 0.014, both
below 0.05. This conservative behavior, together with low power at small CMI,
weakens any interpretation of non-rejection as evidence of no information.}

All 12 scale--sample combinations reached 80\% power within the tested
0.12-bit grid. The interpolated 80\% minimum detectable CMI ranged from
0.0236 to 0.1106 bits. At \(N=90\), it was 0.1043, 0.1106, and 0.0747 bits
for 500~m--day, 1~km--day, and 2~km--week, respectively; at \(N=730\),
the corresponding values were 0.0236, 0.0243, and 0.0238 bits. These
values quantify design-specific detection limits and do not define a
scale-free information threshold.

\subsection{Held-Out Prediction and Predictability Gaps}
\label{subsec:results_prediction}

Held-out prediction evaluated whether particular fitted models converted
the augmented information state into lower error on unseen observations.
\rev{The fixed July--December 2022 bicycle-covered test set contained 413
grids observed over 184 days, or 75,992 grid--day observations.}

\rev{Adding the bicycle state reduced the MSE point estimate in five of the 12
city--model comparisons. Three comparisons passed the
multiplicity-adjusted improvement test: the state-mean and Poisson gradient-boosting
models in New York City and the state-mean model in Vancouver.
The remaining model--city combinations showed either negligible change
or higher MSE after the bicycle state was added (Figure~\ref{fig:mse_improvement}).}

Every baseline and bicycle-augmented held-out MSE remained above its
corresponding DELB (Table~\ref{tab:prediction_gap_results}). A positive gap
narrowing means that the model's MSE decreased by more than the estimated
DELB, so the model moved closer to the lower bound. By this definition, the
New York City state-mean and gradient-boosting models narrowed the
Predictability Gap by 0.001 and 0.029 MSE units, respectively. A negative
value means that the DELB decreased by more than the model MSE, so the added
estimated prediction opportunity was not fully realized by that model. This
pattern occurred in all other comparisons.

\rev{For practical interpretation, consider the New York City baseline DELB
of 0.263 and the bicycle-augmented DELB of 0.230. Under the corresponding
estimated distributions, these values are lower bounds for admissible
integer-valued predictors using the stated information sets; they are not
forecasts of the MSE that a fitted model will automatically achieve. The
observed model MSEs near 1.9 therefore indicate substantial nonattainment of
the estimated floor. The 0.034-unit DELB reduction represents additional
estimated prediction opportunity associated with the augmented information
set. It becomes a realized forecasting gain only to the extent that a fitted
model also reduces held-out MSE, which occurred selectively in
Table~\ref{tab:prediction_gap_results}.}

\rev{The estimated DELB declined in every city, whereas held-out MSE improved in
five of the 12 city--model comparisons and passed the multiplicity-adjusted
improvement screen in three. Only the two New York City models narrowed the
estimated Predictability Gap.}

\subsection{Secondary and Robustness Analyses}

\subsubsection{Fano Classification-Loss Comparison}
\label{subsec:results_fano}
All \FanoOracleRows{} population-oracle comparisons satisfied Fano's inequality,
and all empirical held-out modal-classifier errors remained above their
corresponding pretest Fano floors. No point-estimate violations
were observed.

Adding the bicycle state lowered the estimated Miller--Madow Fano floor
in all six city--target comparisons. Reductions ranged from
\FanoFloorReductionMin{} to \FanoFloorReductionMax{}. In contrast, held-out
modal-classifier error increased in all six comparisons alongside more unseen
bicycle-augmented test states.

\rev{Here, ``unseen'' refers to test observations whose complete conditioning
state was absent before July 2022, not to a count of distinct states. For
example, the New York entries 2,058/4,408 are baseline/bicycle-aware fallback
counts out of 36,432 test observations (5.65\%/12.10\%).}

\rev{The two movements share a finite-sample mechanism in this comparison:
splitting a sparse baseline state by bicycle category lowers the estimated
conditional entropy and hence the estimated Fano floor, while increasing
the number of test observations assigned to an unseen augmented state and
the pretest global-mode fallback. The simultaneous floor reduction and
classifier degradation are therefore diagnostic of state refinement under
sparse support; they do not establish a population information gain.}
The Fano and DELB magnitudes are
not directly comparable because they concern different targets and loss
functions. Crime-type estimates were positive but category-specific
prediction gains were sparse, and rolling-origin gains remained concentrated
in selected New York City models. Alternative estimators and specifications
preserved the sign of the DELB point estimate but did not replace
randomization calibration. \rev{Complete local, multiscale, Fano, crime-type,
alternative-specification, descriptive, and rolling-origin results are
grouped in Supplementary Materials, Sections S2--S6 and are treated as
secondary or exploratory evidence.}
