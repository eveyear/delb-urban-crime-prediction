\abstract{Urban forecasting systems increasingly draw on mobility and other
dynamic data, yet improved test accuracy alone cannot show whether an added
source lowers an information-constrained error bound or merely benefits a
particular model. We develop a finite-sample evaluation framework based on
the Discrete Entropy Lower Bound (DELB) for integer-valued prediction. DELB
maps conditional Shannon entropy to an MSE lower bound through the
integer-lattice maximum-entropy envelope. The empirical analysis separately
examines sparse-state estimation bias, evidence from prespecified
randomization schemes, and held-out performance.
We apply the framework to \TotalCrimeEvents{} crime events and
\rev{\TotalBicycleTrips{} flow-eligible bicycle trips} from Washington, DC, New York City, and
Vancouver during 2020--2022, aggregated into \TotalPanelRows{} 1~km grid--day
observations. \rev{In the primary high-dimensional specification, the observed
conditional mutual information did not exceed the temporal, spatial,
circular-shift, or baseline-state-matched reference distributions. The
positive estimated DELB reductions are therefore finite-sample point
estimates, not calibrated evidence of source-specific information: they were
small over the complete crime-supported populations and 7.9\%, 17.3\%, and
18.0\% within bicycle-covered areas. A
lower-cardinality sensitivity detected a small calibrated difference only in
New York City (0.00105 bits above the matched-reference mean; BH-adjusted
\(q=0.003\)), showing that empirical resolution depends on state complexity.}
\rev{In the scale--sample reference experiment, semi-synthetic null rejection was near nominal, whereas sparse-state settings were conservative (Type I error 0.011--0.014) and power was limited for small effects;} held-out gains varied across
cities and models. The results support a staged evaluation of new urban data
sources: the estimated bound change, randomization-based evidence, and
realized forecasting gain should be reported separately.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{From Predictive Performance to an Information-Theoretic Lower Bound}
\label{subsec:intro_prediction_limit}

Predictive modeling addresses two related but fundamentally distinct
questions: how accurately a particular method forecasts future outcomes,
and how much prediction error is unavoidable given the information
available when a forecast is issued. Most empirical forecasting studies
primarily address the first question by comparing the out-of-sample
performance of competing models. Such comparisons are essential for
model selection, but they do not by themselves reveal whether the remaining
error reflects intrinsic uncertainty or limitations of the fitted predictor.

A model-independent lower bound addresses the latter issue. For a fixed
target, causal information set, loss function, and predictor class, it is
determined by the joint distribution of the target and the information
available at forecast time. It is therefore independent of
the architecture, optimizer, and finite training sample used to construct
a predictor. Comparing achieved prediction error with such a model-independent
bound separates uncertainty intrinsic to the target--information pair from
error that may remain reducible through improved modeling or estimation.

In this study, \textit{predictability limit} denotes the broader research
question. The DELB is the information-theoretic MSE lower bound implied by
conditional uncertainty, integer support, squared-error loss, and the
explicitly defined class of causal integer-valued predictors. It is not
the Bayes risk in general. Equality requires an optimal predictor to exist
and to satisfy the attainability conditions established below. This
distinction is particularly important for
crime counts and other operational outcomes that take values on an
integer lattice, because continuous entropy--variance arguments do
not directly provide the corresponding discrete error floor.
We therefore ask:

\emph{RQ1: How can conditional uncertainty be translated into an exact discrete-form,
    model-independent MSE lower bound for a causal integer-valued
    stochastic process?}

\subsection{Added Information and Finite-Sample Identification}
\label{subsec:intro_finite_sample}

Predictability also depends on what is known at forecast time. When side
information reduces conditional entropy, its population conditional mutual
information (CMI) with the target orders the corresponding DELB. The question
is then whether a new data source changes what is predictable, not merely
whether one fitted model uses it.

Population ordering does not by itself solve finite-sample identification.
Adding a discretized variable partitions each baseline state and can produce
sparse or singleton cells. Plug-in and bias-corrected conditional-entropy
estimators can then exhibit unequal finite-sample errors across the nested
states. The broader entropy-estimation literature shows that undersampling can
produce substantial bias and misleadingly narrow uncertainty assessments even
for nominally corrected estimators \citep{paninski2003,calcagnile2026benchmark}.
CMI estimation and conditional-independence testing add further dependence on
data type, neighborhood or binning structure, and null-generation mechanism
\citep{zan2022cmi,runge2018cmi,li2023nncit}.

A randomization reference distribution recomputes the statistic after a
specified transformation. Its exactness depends on the
exchangeability induced by that design \citep{ernst2004permutation}; in sparse
nested tables, the transformation may also alter support occupancy and the
finite-sample center. Positive estimated CMI, estimator bias, and separation
from a design-conditioned randomization reference are distinct
claims. Our second question is:

\emph{RQ2: How can a population information gain be distinguished from
finite-sample entropy-estimation bias and a transformation-specific
randomization reference?}

We compare a baseline crime-history and calendar
information set with an otherwise matched set augmented by lagged bicycle
flow. Existing studies mainly ask whether adding mobility changes a fitted
model's score. Here, the unresolved question is whether the added
source changes a model-independent error floor and whether an estimated change
remains distinguishable after finite-sample calibration. The third question
concerns evidence rather than causation:

\emph{RQ3: Does lagged bicycle mobility provide additional crime-prediction
information that remains distinguishable after finite-sample and
randomization calibration?}

\subsection{Contributions and Evidence Strategy}
\label{subsec:intro_contributions}

This study makes three contributions spanning theory, finite-sample
inference, and empirical evaluation.

First, we formulate the Discrete Entropy Lower Bound (DELB) for causal
integer-valued prediction. By inverting the exact integer-lattice
maximum-entropy envelope, the DELB converts conditional Shannon entropy
into a universal MSE lower bound for any admissible integer-valued
predictor. Within a unified theorem package, we further derive a
predictor-dependent refinement, establish necessary and sufficient
conditions for attaining the universal bound, and decompose nonattainment
into two distinct components: residual dependence of the prediction error
on the available history and mismatch between the error distribution and
the moment-matched discrete Gaussian. The theoretical contribution lies
in integrating these elements into a causal prediction framework with
explicit diagnostic interpretations. We do not claim the discrete-Gaussian
extremizer, the lattice correction, or the underlying information-projection
identities as new mathematical primitives.

Second, we develop a finite-sample evidence framework for determining
whether an estimated reduction in conditional entropy represents genuine
added predictive information. The framework explicitly distinguishes five
objects that are often conflated: the population DELB and conditional
mutual information estimands, their finite-sample estimates, estimator
behavior under sparse state occupancy, transformation-specific
randomization references, and achieved out-of-sample prediction error.
Known-truth simulations, support diagnostics, block resampling, temporal
and spatial randomization, circular-shift references, and matched
prediction experiments are combined to evaluate these objects without
treating any single diagnostic as sufficient evidence of population
information gain. This framework clarifies why a positive estimated
CMI or a lower estimated DELB need not imply that the added information
is distinguishable from finite-sample artifacts.

Third, we apply the framework to harmonized crime and shared-bicycle data
from Washington, DC, New York City, and Vancouver during 2020--2022.
The empirical analysis evaluates whether lagged bicycle activity provides
predictive information about future integer-valued crime counts beyond
crime history, location, and calendar state. It further examines how DELB
and CMI estimates vary with spatial resolution, temporal aggregation, and
sample size. Bicycle-augmented information sets produce lower DELB point
estimates across the primary city-level comparisons and multiple alternative
specifications. However, the prespecified randomization designs do not
distinguish these reductions from transformation-specific finite-sample
references, and no recent bicycle-specific reduction in the population
DELB is identified after calibration. This qualified
result is substantively important: an apparent reduction in estimated
uncertainty, statistically distinguishable added information, and improved
realized prediction are separate empirical claims.

The evidence strategy follows the same sequence as the research questions.
Section~\ref{sec:related_work} positions the study relative to urban crime
forecasting, mobility-based crime analytics, and information-theoretic
prediction limits. Section~\ref{sec:theory} develops the population DELB
and answers RQ1. Section~\ref{sec:data} defines the data, estimands,
finite-sample diagnostics, randomization designs, and matched prediction
procedures required for RQ2 and RQ3. Section~\ref{sec:results}
first establishes the finite-sample behavior of the estimators and then
evaluates the city-level and multiscale evidence.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to the three research questions,
discusses the scope of the resulting information-theoretic lower bound, and identifies
the implications for integer-valued prediction more broadly.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Crime Prediction}
\label{subsec:intro_crime_prediction}

Forecasting when and where crime will occur is an established objective of
spatial crime analysis. Forecasts may concern future incident counts,
hotspot membership, crime categories, or event locations, and they are used
to support the temporal and geographic allocation of limited public-safety
resources. The available literature spans conventional statistical and
spatial models as well as increasingly flexible data-driven methods. A
systematic review of spatial crime forecasting identified hotspot
classification as the predominant task and documented a rapid expansion in
the range of algorithms applied to it \citep{kounadi2020review}. This
methodological development has made it possible to combine historical crime
records with calendar, socioeconomic, built-environment, and other
spatiotemporal covariates at progressively finer resolutions.

Representative studies illustrate the movement from statistical learning
toward more elaborate temporal and spatial representations. Random forests
have been used to relate urban indicators to crime rates
\citep{alves2018crime}; boosted-tree and XGBoost-based specifications have
been incorporated into spatiotemporal crime-prediction systems
\citep{angbera2023model}; and recurrent architectures, including LSTM-based
models, have been used to represent evolving crime dependencies
\citep{huang2018deepcrime}. More recently, transformer-based formulations
have been proposed to capture longer-range spatiotemporal structure
\citep{butt2025start}. These examples are important here not because of
their internal architectures, but because each represents a different
attempt to reduce forecast error by improving the mapping from an available
information set to a future crime outcome.

The principal criterion for judging such attempts remains predictive
performance. In the review by Kounadi et al., prediction accuracy was the
most frequently reported evaluation measure, followed by the prediction
accuracy index and the F1 score \citep{kounadi2020review}. Count-forecasting
studies additionally use measures such as MAE, RMSE, and MSE. These metrics
are indispensable for comparing models under a common evaluation design,
but a ranking of observed errors answers only which evaluated model performs
better. It does not reveal how much uncertainty is intrinsic to the target
process under the specified predictors, how close the best model is to a
fundamental limit, or whether further architectural complexity can
meaningfully reduce the remaining error. Consequently, an accuracy-centered
literature can document continual model improvement while leaving the
absolute predictability of crime counts unresolved.

\subsection{From Prediction Accuracy to Predictability Limits}
\label{subsec:intro_predictability_limits}

A prediction error and a predictability limit are conceptually different.
The former is achieved by a particular estimator, training sample, and
optimization procedure. The latter is a property of the joint distribution
of the prediction target and the information legitimately available when
the forecast is issued. For a fixed information set and loss function, the
limit describes a model-independent error floor: changing the forecasting
algorithm alone cannot cross it. This distinction converts the usual
question of whether one model outperforms another into a more fundamental
question: how much of the observed error is irreducible given the available
information, and how much remains  potentially recoverable by a better
predictor?

Information theory provides a natural language for this question. Shannon
entropy ≥ uncertainty in a random variable, while conditional entropy
measures the uncertainty that remains after an information set is observed
\citep{shannon1948,cover_thomas}. Entropy-based limits have already clarified
predictability in other complex systems. Song et al. combined the entropy of
individual trajectories with Fano-type reasoning to estimate the potential
predictability of human mobility \citep{song2010limits}. Li et al. used
conditional differential entropy to study model-independent performance
boundaries in short-term traffic forecasting \citep{li2022traffic}. At a
more general level, Fang et al. derived variance bounds for estimation and
prediction errors of continuous-valued time series from differential-entropy
arguments \citep{fang_variance_bounds}. Collectively, these studies show why
accuracy should be interpreted relative to the uncertainty contained in the
target and its conditioning information.

Crime counts, however, are integer-valued rather than continuous. A
continuous variance bound cannot be converted into a discrete result by
mechanically replacing differential entropy with Shannon entropy. The
continuous Gaussian maximizes differential entropy under a variance
constraint, whereas an integer-valued error is supported on a lattice and
has a different maximum-entropy distribution and entropy--moment relation.
The distinction matters especially for sparse local counts, for which the
lattice correction is not negligible. To the best of our knowledge, a
model-independent MSE floor derived from the exact discrete maximum-entropy
envelope has received limited attention in crime forecasting.

This study therefore introduces the term \emph{Discrete Entropy Lower Bound}
(DELB) for an MSE lower bound constructed specifically for integer-valued
prediction errors. The exact bound connects conditional Shannon entropy to
the maximum-entropy distribution on the integer lattice. The proposed
four-part theorem package places in one causal prediction statement
(i) the exact inverse-lattice floor, (ii) a predictor-dependent refinement,
(iii) necessary and sufficient attainability conditions, and (iv) a
two-component decomposition of nonattainment. A conservative closed-form
version exposes the corresponding lattice correction. Once the floor and
an out-of-sample MSE are estimated under matched information sets, their
difference defines a \emph{Predictability Gap}. This gap is intended to
separate estimated irreducible uncertainty from the portion of error that
current models have not exploited; it is not another model-ranking score.
The full definitions and finite-sample qualifications are developed in
Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\subsection{Bicycle Mobility as Additional Predictive Information}
\label{subsec:intro_bicycle_information}

Predictability depends not only on the forecasting rule but also on what is
known at forecast time. This observation is central to the relationship
between mobility and crime. Routine activity theory links crime
opportunities to the convergence in space and time of potential offenders,
suitable targets, and the absence of capable guardians
\citep{cohen1979routine}. Residential population is therefore an incomplete
description of the population at risk in places whose users change over the
day. Research on ambient population has shown that alternative measures of
the people actually present can materially alter the identification and
interpretation of urban crime hotspots \citep{malleson2016ambient}.

Digital traces have made these dynamic populations and flows increasingly
observable. Mobile-phone trajectories indicate that everyday mobility flows
help explain thieves' target-location choices \citep{song2019legal}.
Foursquare activity, subway use, and taxi trips have been used to improve
long-term crime prediction beyond models based on census and
points-of-interest variables \citep{kadar2018mining}. Directional mobility
flows derived from location platforms have also been linked to hourly crime
concentrations in multiple cities \citep{kadar2020mobility}. At shorter
horizons, adding mobility-flow features to historical crime inputs has
improved F1 scores across several cities, crime categories, and prediction
architectures \citep{wu2022mobility}. The common implication is that dynamic
urban activity can contain predictive information that static residential
or built-environment measures do not capture.

Bicycle records offer a distinct source of such information. Shared-bicycle
and bicycle-count data provide high-frequency timestamps and spatially
resolved origins, destinations, inflows, and outflows. Dockless bike-sharing
trips have been used to reveal intra-urban mobility structures and daily
activity patterns \citep{xu2023bike}, while comparisons with bicycle
counters suggest that sharing-system records can reproduce important
temporal patterns in overall cycling activity at finer spatial granularity
\citep{ivanovic2023bike}. In the present study, bicycle flow is consequently
treated as a \emph{mode-specific, high-frequency proxy for urban mobility
and public-space activity}. It is not treated as a complete measure of the
ambient population: bicycle users are a selected subset of urban travelers,
and service areas, station placement, weather, and operating conditions
affect observed coverage.

Existing evidence that mobility variables improve a fitted model does not
identify how much conditional information they add or how they change a
model-independent error floor. An observed gain may depend on the chosen
architecture, regularization, feature encoding, or sample split. We instead
compare a baseline information set with an otherwise matched information
set augmented by lagged bicycle flows. Conditional mutual information then
quantifies the remaining association between future crime counts and
bicycle mobility after crime history and prespecified controls are known,
and its entropy reduction determines the ordering of the associated DELBs.
This quantity describes the predictive information value of observed
mobility; it neither identifies a causal effect of bicycle travel on crime
nor guarantees that every forecasting model will realize the same empirical
improvement.

The distinction is especially important when a richer conditioning state is
sparsely occupied. A positive empirical entropy difference may combine
population information, unequal finite-sample bias between nested state
tables, and random error. Moreover, a randomization null is the distribution
of the same estimator after a specified transformation; its center need not
be zero when the transformed support remains sparse. Accordingly, the
population estimand, estimator bias, and transformation-specific null are
treated as separate inferential layers rather than interchangeable evidence.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{subsec:intro_contributions}

Against this background, the study makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate and prove a four-part causal integer-prediction
    theorem package comprising the exact inverse-lattice DELB, a
    predictor-dependent refinement, necessary and sufficient attainability
    conditions, and a two-component prediction-slack diagnostic. The
    discrete-Gaussian extremizer, exponential-family KL identity, and
    continuous entropy--error argument are explicitly treated as inherited
    ingredients rather than separate novelty claims.
    \item We establish a reproducible finite-sample inference framework that
    separates the population DELB/CMI estimand, its plug-in or
    Miller--Madow estimate, estimator bias, the design-conditioned
    randomization null, and achieved out-of-sample error. Simulations and
    surrogate-specific support diagnostics quantify why these layers need
    not agree.
    \item We connect the count-MSE DELB to the Predictability Gap and to the
    classical Fano floor for fixed finite labels, while keeping their
    targets, losses, equality conditions, and numerical scales distinct.
    \item We evaluate the framework using harmonized property-crime and
    bicycle-flow panels for Vancouver, Washington, DC, and New York City,
    reporting both robust point-estimate patterns and the absence of
    bicycle-specific randomization evidence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods and Experimental Design}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Discretization of Count and Mobility Variables}
\label{subsec:discretization}

The primary prediction target remains the original theft count
\begin{equation}
C_{c,i,t}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0};
\end{equation}
it is not binned in the main analysis. To obtain a finite, sufficiently
populated conditioning state, the lagged count is mapped by
\begin{equation}
Q_C(C_{c,i,t-\ell})\in\{0,1,2,\geq3\},
\end{equation}
with \(\{0,1,\geq2\}\) as a robustness mapping. The primary bicycle-state
mapping preserves the substantively meaningful zero state and divides
positive lagged total flow using city-specific training-period quantiles:
\begin{equation}
Q_B(B^{\mathrm{total}}_{c,i,t-\ell})
\in\{0,\text{low},\text{medium},\text{high}\}.
\end{equation}
Calendar, weather, and spatial controls are similarly mapped by \(Q_X\)
when they are not already categorical. All cut points are estimated using
the training period only and then applied unchanged to validation and test
periods. City-specific quantile bins constitute the primary specification;
common normalized bins are used for cross-city robustness. Deterministic
top-coding of the target itself is evaluated only as a robustness analysis,
because it yields the conservative entropy ordering in
Equation~\eqref{eq:top_coded_entropy_order}.

\subsection{Lag Selection and Information Sets}
\label{subsec:lag_selection}

The primary local entropy specification uses \(p=q=1\) and the
low-dimensional sigma-algebras
\begin{align}
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{c,i,t}
&=
\sigma\!\left\{
Q_C(C_{c,i,t-1}),
\operatorname{DOW}_t,
\operatorname{Season}_t,
Q_X(\mathbf{W}_{c,t})
\right\},
\label{eq:empirical_baseline_information}\\
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{c,i,t}
&=
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{c,i,t}
\vee
\sigma\!\left\{
Q_B(B^{\mathrm{total}}_{c,i,t-1})
\right\}.
\label{eq:empirical_bicycle_information}
\end{align}
Here, \(\mathbf{W}_{c,t}\) denotes the weather controls. These empirical
states instantiate Equations~\eqref{eq:baseline_information} and
\eqref{eq:bike_information}. Their parsimonious form limits sparse-cell bias
in local entropy estimation while retaining the raw count as the outcome.

Robustness specifications should consider:
\begin{itemize}
    \item crime lags $p\in\{1,2,3,7\}$;
    \item bicycle lags $q\in\{1,2,3,7\}$;
    \item a weekly seasonal lag $C_{t-7}$;
    \item inbound, outbound, total, and net bicycle flows;
    \item same-day bicycle activity as a clearly labeled nowcasting analysis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Entropy Estimation}
\label{subsec:entropy_estimation}

For each city and eligible spatial unit, the outcome atoms in the empirical
state counts are the observed raw values of \(C_{c,i,t}\); only the
conditioning variables are mapped into the finite states described above.
The plug-in estimator is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{PI}}(X)
=-\sum_{x:\widehat{p}(x)>0}\widehat{p}(x)\log_2\widehat{p}(x).
\end{equation}
The primary finite-sample correction may be the Miller--Madow estimator,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{MM}}(X)
=
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{PI}}(X)
+
\frac{K_X-1}{2N\ln 2},
\label{eq:miller_madow}
\end{equation}
where $K_X$ is the number of observed nonzero-probability states and $N$ is the sample size. A Bayesian Dirichlet estimator is used as an alternative.

Conditional entropy is estimated as
\begin{equation}
\widehat{H}(C\mid Y)
=
\widehat{H}(C,Y)-\widehat{H}(Y),
\end{equation}
and conditional mutual information as
\begin{equation}
\widehat{I}(C;B\mid Y)
=
\widehat{H}(C\mid Y)-\widehat{H}(C\mid Y,B).
\label{eq:estimated_cmi}
\end{equation}
Because separate bias corrections can occasionally produce small negative estimates, report the raw value for diagnostic purposes and use a prespecified nonnegative projection only when inserting the estimate into a quantity that is theoretically nonnegative. The choice must be stated explicitly.

The exact inverse-envelope DELB is the primary theoretical estimand. For a
nonnegative conditional-entropy estimate \(\widehat{H}_{+}\), solve
\begin{equation}
\frac{
\widehat{\lambda}D(\widehat{\lambda})
+
\ln\Theta(\widehat{\lambda})
}{\ln2}
=
\widehat{H}_{+}
\label{eq:numerical_inverse_envelope}
\end{equation}
and set
\begin{equation}
\widehat{L}
=
D(\widehat{\lambda}).
\label{eq:estimated_exact_delb}
\end{equation}
When \(\widehat{H}_{+}=0\), define \(\widehat{L}=0\). Numerical sums over
the integer lattice are expanded until their omitted tail mass is below a
prespecified tolerance. The closed-form value in
Equation~\eqref{eq:discrete_mse_bound} is reported as a secondary,
conservative result rather than as an attainable approximation to the exact
bound.

\subsection{City-Level Information-Theoretic Experiment}
\label{subsec:city_level_experiment}

\textbf{Objective:} Determine whether lagged bicycle mobility reduces theft uncertainty and the corresponding theoretical MSE lower bound at the city level.

\textbf{Procedure:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Construct a harmonized grid--day panel for each city.
    \item Define the baseline and mobility-augmented information sets.
    \item Estimate \(H(C\mid\mathcal{F}^{0})\), \(H(C\mid\mathcal{F}^{B})\), and \(I(C;Q_B(\mathbf{B}^{(q)})\mid\mathcal{F}^{0})\).
    \item Calculate the exact \(L^{0}\), \(L^{B}\), \(\Delta L\), and \(R^{L}\) using Equations~\eqref{eq:local_bound_baseline}--\eqref{eq:relative_bound_reduction}; report the closed-form counterparts as secondary results.
    \item Repeat for each year separately and for the pooled 2020--2022 period.
    \item Estimate uncertainty using the block-bootstrap method described in Section~\ref{subsec:bootstrap}.
\end{enumerate}

The analysis may be implemented either by pooling eligible grid--day observations within a city while conditioning on spatial-unit identity, or by aggregating the local estimates using prespecified weights. Both definitions should not be mixed; the chosen city-level estimand must be stated clearly.

\subsection{Spatially Localized Bound-Reduction Experiment}
\label{subsec:local_experiment}

\textbf{Objective:} Identify where bicycle mobility produces the largest reduction in the theoretical prediction-error lower bound.

For every eligible spatial unit $i$, calculate
\begin{equation}
\widehat{I}^{B}_{c,i}
=
\widehat{I}\!\left(
C_{c,i,t};
Q_B(\mathbf{B}^{(q)}_{c,i,t})
\mid\mathcal{F}^{0}_{c,i,t}
\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\Delta L}_{c,i}
=
\widehat{L}^{0}_{c,i}-\widehat{L}^{B}_{c,i},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\widehat{R}^{L}_{c,i}
=
\frac{\widehat{L}^{0}_{c,i}-\widehat{L}^{B}_{c,i}}
{\widehat{L}^{0}_{c,i}},
\end{equation}
where \(\widehat{R}^{L}_{c,i}\) is reported only when
\(\widehat{L}^{0}_{c,i}>0\).

\textbf{Procedure for each spatial unit:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Extract its complete daily sequence for 2020--2022.
    \item Generate lagged crime and bicycle states without crossing missing-date boundaries.
    \item Apply training-derived discretization thresholds.
    \item Estimate baseline and mobility-conditioned entropy.
    \item Convert the entropy estimates into lower bounds.
    \item Estimate confidence intervals by moving-block bootstrap.
    \item Apply false-discovery-rate correction across spatial units.
    \item Map local CMI, absolute reduction, relative reduction, and statistical significance.
\end{enumerate}

The principal local maps are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item local conditional mutual information $\widehat{I}^{B}_{c,i}$;
    \item absolute lower-bound reduction $\widehat{\Delta L}_{c,i}$;
    \item relative lower-bound reduction $\widehat{R}^{L}_{c,i}$;
    \item statistically significant reduction after false-discovery-rate correction.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Block Bootstrap and Multiple Testing}
\label{subsec:bootstrap}

Temporal dependence invalidates an independent and identically distributed bootstrap. We therefore use a moving-block or stationary bootstrap. Let $\ell$ denote the block length. The primary analysis uses $\ell=7$ days, with $\ell\in\{7,14,28\}$ evaluated for robustness.

For each city or spatial unit:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Form overlapping blocks of length $\ell$ from the complete time series.
    \item Sample blocks with replacement until a bootstrap series of length at least $T$ is obtained.
    \item Truncate the concatenated series to length $T$.
    \item Recalculate entropy, CMI, and lower-bound statistics.
    \item Repeat $R=1000$ times, or use $R=500$ during development and $R=1000$ for final results.
    \item Construct percentile or bias-corrected confidence intervals.
\end{enumerate}

A spatial unit is classified as having a positive mobility-induced lower-bound reduction when the lower endpoint of its confidence interval for $\Delta L_{c,i}$ exceeds zero. Benjamini--Hochberg correction controls the false discovery rate across local tests within each city.

\subsection{Spatial Clustering and Area Characteristics}
\label{subsec:spatial_explanation}

Global Moran's $I$ is calculated for $\widehat{I}^{B}_{c,i}$, $\widehat{\Delta L}_{c,i}$, and $\widehat{R}^{L}_{c,i}$ using a prespecified spatial weights matrix. Local Moran's $I$ or Getis--Ord $G_i^{*}$ is used to identify clusters of high and low mobility information value.

To characterize high-value areas with \(L^{0}_{c,i}>0\), estimate an explanatory model of the form
\begin{equation}
R^{L}_{c,i}
=
\alpha_c
+
\beta_1\overline{B}_{c,i}
+
\beta_2\operatorname{Var}(B_{c,i,t})
+
\beta_3\overline{C}_{c,i}
+
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{c,i}
+
\epsilon_{c,i},
\label{eq:area_characteristics_model}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Z}_{c,i}$ may contain station density, population or employment density, land-use mixture, point-of-interest density, transit accessibility, and distance to the central business district. Results are interpreted associationally rather than causally. Spatial-error or spatial-lag specifications may be used when residual spatial dependence is substantial.

\subsection{Predictive Models}
\label{subsec:predictive_models}

The prediction experiments test whether theoretical information gains translate into out-of-sample performance gains. The following models provide a balance between count-process interpretation and nonlinear predictive flexibility:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item seasonal naive baseline;
    \item Poisson regression;
    \item negative-binomial regression;
    \item gradient-boosted decision trees, such as LightGBM or XGBoost.
\end{enumerate}

For the primary theory--practice comparison, every model uses two matched
categorical information specifications:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{X}^{0}_{c,i,t}
&=
\{Q_C(C_{c,i,t-1:t-p}),\operatorname{DOW}_t,
\operatorname{Season}_t,Q_X(\mathbf{W}_{c,t})\},\\
\mathcal{X}^{B}_{c,i,t}
&=
\mathcal{X}^{0}_{c,i,t}
\cup
\{Q_B(B^{\mathrm{total}}_{c,i,t-1:t-q})\}.
\end{align}
Inbound, outbound, net, and primitive two-dimensional flow specifications are
fitted separately and are not redundantly combined with their deterministic
summaries. Hyperparameters must be selected using only the training and
validation periods. Predictions compared directly with
Theorem~\ref{thm:exact_delb} are converted to nonnegative integers using a
prespecified rounding rule. Continuous conditional-mean predictions and
models using richer raw covariates may also be evaluated, but their empirical
MSE is not described as being covered by the matched integer-predictor DELB;
each richer information set has its own theoretical floor.

\subsection{Temporal Validation Design}
\label{subsec:validation}

Random train--test splitting is not used. A simple primary split is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item training: January 2020--December 2021;
    \item validation: January 2022--June 2022;
    \item testing: July 2022--December 2022.
\end{itemize}
Because 2020 contains substantial pandemic-related disruption, rolling-origin evaluation is used as a principal robustness design:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item train on all observations available before a forecast month;
    \item predict the next month;
    \item advance the origin by one month;
    \item aggregate predictions and errors across all test months.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Prediction Metrics and Theory--Practice Comparison}
\label{subsec:metrics}

The primary prediction metric is MSE because the theoretical result is an MSE lower bound:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MSE}
=
\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{test}}}
\sum_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{test}}}
\left(C_{c,i,t}-\widehat{C}_{c,i,t}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
Auxiliary metrics include RMSE, MAE, Poisson deviance, and calibration by predicted-count interval. MAPE is not used as a primary metric because theft counts include many zeros.

For each spatial unit, define the empirical improvement
\begin{equation}
\Delta\operatorname{MSE}_{c,i}
=
\operatorname{MSE}^{0}_{c,i}-\operatorname{MSE}^{B}_{c,i}.
\label{eq:mse_improvement}
\end{equation}
The relationship between theoretical and empirical improvements is evaluated using Spearman correlation, city-specific bootstrap confidence intervals, and pooled regressions with city fixed effects.

Spatial units are classified into four descriptive types:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item high $\Delta L$ and high $\Delta\operatorname{MSE}$: informative mobility successfully exploited by the model;
    \item high $\Delta L$ and low $\Delta\operatorname{MSE}$: information exists but is not fully exploited;
    \item low $\Delta L$ and high $\Delta\operatorname{MSE}$: possible estimator uncertainty, regularization effects, or model-specific gains;
    \item low $\Delta L$ and low $\Delta\operatorname{MSE}$: limited local mobility information value.
\end{enumerate}
Thresholds for ``high'' and ``low'' must be defined before mapping, for example using city-specific medians or statistical significance criteria.

\subsection{Placebo and Robustness Experiments}
\label{subsec:robustness_methods}

\subsubsection{Temporal Block Permutation}
Within each spatial unit, permute bicycle blocks while approximately preserving short-range dependence. Recalculate CMI and bound reduction. The observed result should be compared with the placebo distribution.

\subsubsection{Spatial Permutation}
Randomly reassign complete bicycle time series among spatial units within the same city. This tests whether the result reflects local matching rather than only a city-wide seasonal mobility pattern.

\subsubsection{Distant-Lag Placebo}
Replace recent bicycle activity with distant lags, such as $B_{t-14}$ and $B_{t-30}$. Similar effects at implausibly distant lags would indicate residual shared seasonality.

\subsubsection{Surrogate Mobility Series}
Generate surrogate bicycle series preserving selected marginal and autocorrelation properties but breaking the crime--mobility relationship. Compare observed $\Delta L$ with its surrogate distribution.

\subsubsection{Alternative Scales and Estimators}
Repeat the analysis using alternative spatial resolutions, daily and weekly
aggregation, conditioning-state mappings, target top-coding as a separate
robustness check, lag lengths, eligibility thresholds, entropy estimators,
and bootstrap block lengths.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Main Information-Theoretic Findings}
Interpret whether bicycle mobility contains information about theft beyond crime history and common temporal and weather drivers. Distinguish unconditional association, conditional information gain, theoretical lower-bound reduction, and actual forecasting improvement.

\subsection{Where Bicycle Mobility Is Most Informative}
Discuss the geography of local CMI and lower-bound reduction. Compare mobility-information hotspots with theft-count hotspots and explain why the two need not coincide. Relate high-value areas to mobility intensity, volatility, commercial activity, station coverage, and urban structure, while avoiding causal language unless a causal design is added.

\subsection{Theory versus Predictive Practice}
Discuss areas where $\Delta L$ is large but $\Delta\operatorname{MSE}$ is small. Such areas indicate that relevant information may be present in bicycle data but not extracted by the tested models. Conversely, empirical improvement without a stable estimated bound reduction may reflect finite-sample entropy-estimation error, regularization, or model-specific interactions.

\subsection{Cross-City and Temporal Heterogeneity}
Interpret differences among Vancouver, Washington, DC, and New York City. Consider bicycle-data coverage, urban morphology, bicycle-system scale, theft composition, reporting practices, and pandemic-related structural change. Analyze 2020, 2021, and 2022 separately before describing a pooled relationship as stable.

\subsection{Methodological Implications}
Explain how the proposed bound complements conventional predictive evaluation. The framework does not replace forecasting experiments; it separates the information theoretically available under an information set from the extent to which a model uses that information.

\subsection{Limitations}
At minimum, discuss:
\begin{itemize}
    \item finite-sample bias and sparse state spaces;
    \item sensitivity to discretization and lag selection;
    \item differences in crime classification and bicycle coverage across cities;
    \item ecological interpretation of grid-level associations;
    \item the effect of pandemic-era structural breaks;
    \item the integer-valued-predictor condition in Theorem~\ref{thm:exact_delb};
    \item the fact that a lower bound need not be tight;
    \item lack of causal identification;
    \item potential underreporting and changes in policing or reporting behavior.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Summarize the theoretical result, the city-level information gains, the spatial areas where mobility most reduces the estimated lower bound, and the relationship between theoretical and empirical prediction improvements. Emphasize that the study evaluates how an added mobility information set changes fundamental predictability rather than merely whether one model's accuracy improves. End with concrete future directions, such as multivariate lattice bounds, event-level temporal analysis, richer mobility modes, and prospective validation beyond 2022.
